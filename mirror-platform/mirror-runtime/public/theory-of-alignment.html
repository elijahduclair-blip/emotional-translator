Theory of Alignment

Meaning lives
in relation.

The Theory of Alignment treats emotion and identity as movement through an environment, not as fixed labels. Color is a tracing system for direction, atmosphere, and relational position. The map stays stable while the person's degree of vision and path through it can change.

Color directions

Nine primary color positions act as whole-number directional anchors. A color is a region of the coordinate space before it is associated with a word, idea, feeling, or relational climate.

Depth inside direction

Shades add resolution inside an existing color family. Words can receive more precise addresses beneath the closest shade. Added detail deepens the map without replacing its foundations.

Climate River

Emotion moves like weather through atmosphere, rooms, crossroads, tenderness, and shadow. A present climate describes an environment; it does not permanently define the person inside it.

Relationship River

Meaning develops between positions: intimacy and realism, masks and exposure, tension and imperfection. A relationship describes the accountable path between locations without redefining either coordinate.

Identity and Pressure River

Identity can form through pressure, movement, reinvention, endurance, regulation, ambiguity, and recovery. Multiple climates or inner positions may coexist.

Degree of vision

The full map can remain available while attention concentrates on one directional field. Degree of vision records what portion is currently being examined; it does not move the map itself.

Accountability before interpretation

Coordinates define location. Labels describe location. Relationships connect locations. Evidence supports a proposed path. Repeated patterns may form a behavioral fingerprint, but they must be tested for consistency and possible falsification instead of being treated as unlimited interpretation.

The map guides translation. It does not diagnose a person, assign permanent emotional meaning, or silently mutate shared knowledge.
